## 2. Setting

### 2.1 Cells, positions and the reference

We consider a decoder-only transformer with $L$ layers, $H$ query heads and $G$ key/value heads per layer, and rotary position embeddings (RoPE) [@su2021roformer]. RoPE rotates queries and keys by their positions through a block-diagonal matrix $R(p)$ with $R(a)R(b)=R(a+b)$ and $R(p)^\top = R(-p)$, so the logit between a query token $i$ at position $\pi_i$ and an earlier token $j$ at position $\pi_j$ depends only on their distance:

$$a_{ij} = \big(R(\pi_i)\,q_i\big)^{\top}\big(R(\pi_j)\,k_j\big) = q_i^{\top} R(\pi_j-\pi_i)\,k_j .$$

Serving engines cache, for every token and layer, the rotated key $\tilde k_j = R(p_j)k_j$ and the value $v_j$, where $p_j$ is the position at which the token was encoded. The reference implementations of the two architectures we use, Qwen2 and Llama, do the same. We call a token's keys and values over all layers its **cell**.

Our reference throughout is the **re-prefill**: an ordinary forward pass over the current token sequence, with positions $0, 1, 2, \dots$ and an empty cache (sometimes called a cold pass). A cached cell is **exact** if it equals the cell the re-prefill would produce for that token, and an output is correct if it equals the re-prefill's output. Zhang and Yang use the same definition for rollback [@zhang2026aborted]; we apply it to edits anywhere in the context. Equal here means equal up to floating-point rounding: in our engine, the same re-prefill split differently between cache and new input differs by up to {{e1p.qwen1.5b.consistency}} nats.

### 2.2 Edits, readers and the two signals

We study one edit at a time and write the context as $P \cdot E \cdot S$, followed by new input $Q$. The prefix $P$ is unaffected. $E$ is the edited segment: a **delete** removes it, an **insert** places it between $P$ and $S$ after $S$ was encoded, and a replacement is a delete followed by an insert. The suffix $S$ was encoded under the old context, so after a delete its cells still carry what they read from $E$, and after an insert they lack it. $Q$ is whatever is computed next, whether a user turn, a tool result or only the tokens that open the assistant's reply.

Section 3 moves the cells of $S$ to their new positions exactly; no rotation can fix what they read. A system can also leave positions alone and mask the deleted span, leaving a gap [@vllm58537], which Section 4.3 shows is an approximation of its own.

For a cached token $j \in S$ we use two quantities, each averaged over layers $\ell$ and heads $h$. Its **exposure** $m_j = \frac{1}{LH}\sum_{\ell,h} m^{(\ell,h)}_j$ is the attention mass it put on $E$ when it was encoded. After a delete this mass was computed during prefill and can be recorded then; after an insert it has to be estimated. Its **reader attention** $\rho_j = \frac{1}{LH|Q|}\sum_{\ell,h}\sum_{r \in Q} \alpha^{(\ell,h)}_{rj}$ is the attention the new input puts on it, measured by one pass of $Q$ over the stale cache. The readers of the edit score high on both, and a question asked before the edit is the typical case.

### 2.3 Models, data and metrics

**Models and engine.** Most experiments use three instruction-tuned models that run on one laptop in 32-bit floats: Qwen2.5-0.5B, Qwen2.5-1.5B [@qwen25] and SmolLM2-1.7B [@smollm2]{{scope.7b_setting}}. Contexts come from HotpotQA [@yang2018hotpotqa] and WikiText-103 [@merity2017wikitext]. Everything runs on the GPU of an Apple M5 laptop with 24 GB of memory, and identities are checked in 64-bit floats on the CPU. We wrote our own forward pass on the published weights, so that any subset of a sequence can be recomputed while the rest is read from cache at any position. In 32-bit floats its logits differ from the reference implementation's by at most {{e0.library_gap}} on 512-token inputs; in bfloat16 it agrees with the library's bfloat16 pass about as often as that pass agrees with the library's own 32-bit one (Appendix F).

**Fidelity.** We compare the next-token distributions of the re-prefill and of the state with reused cells by $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{re-prefill}} \,\Vert\, p_{\text{reuse}})$, in nats. Section 5 averages this KL over the re-prefill's own reply, teacher-forced, for up to 16 tokens. Our question-answering replies are short (a median of {{e2c.qwen1.5b.reply_median}} tokens on Qwen2.5-1.5B), so the first reply token weighs heavily; Appendix C also reports the KL without it and with replies of up to 64 tokens. Sections 4 and 6 use the KL at the first reply token. We also decode 16 tokens greedily and count how often the reply matches the re-prefill's. Accuracy against the dataset's gold answers cannot rank the policies here: with a supporting document deleted, the re-prefill itself contains the gold answer in only {{acc.contains.truth.range}} of replies, and {{acc.policies_vs_truth}} (Table {{#C:task}}).

**What counts as small.** Two references put a KL in scale. Swapping two documents that do not support the answer, in an exact re-prefill, moves the reply by {{e2c.qwen1.5b.noise.kl_tf}} nats on average on Qwen2.5-1.5B (median {{e2c.qwen1.5b.noise.kl_tf.median}}). Computing the same re-prefill in bfloat16 instead of 32-bit floats moves it by {{e17.qwen1.5b.fp32_vs_bf16}}, {{e17.qwen1.5b.vs_repair}} what a 10% repair by the bridge score leaves ({{e2c.qwen1.5b.kl_tf.bridge.10}}). We therefore compare policies only within one number format, against a re-prefill computed in that format.

**Statistics.** Brackets give 95% bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap intervals of means from 4,000 resamples, or Wilson intervals of proportions. Each question appears in several layouts, so intervals resample whole questions, and paired Wilcoxon tests compare per-question means, Holm-corrected within the family of comparisons a table names. To call two policies close we use the ratio of their mean KLs with a bootstrap interval, never a test that failed to reject. KL is heavy-tailed, with means several times the medians, so Appendix C also gives medians and per-case distributions. We designed the combined policies on the main experiment, so it is exploratory for comparisons among them. The replication in Section 5.3 is confirmatory: its hypotheses were written down before it ran, and its decision rules were fixed before the results of two of its three models were examined (Appendix D). All models see the same questions, so agreement across models is not independent replication.
