## 7. Related work

**Repairing a cache after an edit.** This is the closest line of work, and most of it appeared in the months before this paper. Mao et al. formulate repair after a length-preserving factual edit as budgeted recomputation [@mao2026contiguity]. They compare an edit-local window, structural delimiters, stale-query attention, CacheBlend's deviation and random selection under actual recomputation, find that the window wins when the dependent text is adjacent to the edit and loses most of its advantage when that text moves downstream, and show that positions chosen by transplanting clean states can fail when they are recomputed. Yu et al. study deletions in agent contexts [@yu2026budgeted]. Their method refreshes whole text regions ranked by embedding relevance to the next query, with a packed executor, and they report that with identical token scores, refreshing regions recovers far more answers than refreshing isolated tokens. Among their fourteen allocators, one scores each retained token by its attention to the deleted span, which is our exposure, and another by query attention, which is our reader attention. Neither they nor Mao et al. combine the two. Gu et al. find that in rolling agents, which evict old documents and append new ones, document-aligned recomputation is far less sensitive to request history than token top-$k$ [@gu2026requestorder]. Li shows by causal intervention that a changed field's conclusion lives in downstream tokens written at prefill, ranks those tokens by an oracle causal effect, and recommends an appended erratum over recomputation [@li2026notes]. Our bridge score can be read as a cheap, deployable stand-in for that causal ranking.

Other designs keep the suffix and accept the error. PIE re-applies the correct rotary positions to the cached keys after a code edit and keeps their contents [@he2025pie]. Leyline defines span-replacement directives and a splice kernel that corrects rotary phases in closed form while keeping downstream cells as they were [@ma2026leyline]. A vLLM proposal masks removed spans without renumbering [@vllm58537], and context language models reuse the suffix after correcting its positions [@shao2026clm]. KVEraser trains a low-rank adapter that writes steering states into an erased span so that the unchanged suffix behaves as if the span were gone [@li2026kveraser]. Zhou et al. compare hiding and recomputing after a fact changes and find that later states also carry useful history [@zhou2026positions]. Zhang and Yang define rollback integrity as equality with a cold pass over the committed transcript and leave interior erasure open [@zhang2026aborted], and Yao et al. prove that exact unlearning in a stateful agent needs the suffix after the forgotten content replayed [@yao2026forgetting]. Against this work we add a measurement of where the error sits, a selector that combines the two signals the error's path suggests, and tests of when windows and whole-segment recomputation work and fail. Our results on position gaps, key rewriting and erasure apply to the designs that keep the suffix.

**Selective recomputation for composed caches.** A larger body of work reuses the KV of documents encoded separately and recomputes some tokens to restore the attention between them. The tokens are chosen by first-layer deviation [@yao2025cacheblend], by position at the start of each document [@hu2025epic], by recorded inter-chunk attention [@agarwal2025cachecraft], by the query's attention [@wang2026prophetkv], by an attention-norm signal from the query under consistent positions [@teng2026infoflow], by deviations found in prefill and decode across tenants [@yang2025kvshare], or as whole attention heads classified offline [@liu2026redknot]. RelaxKV also restricts which context each recomputed token reads [@qi2026relaxkv]. SAFE-KV re-encodes the causal suffix from a selected document [@zhang2026safekv], and Peng et al. find that the budget is best spent on the chunk that holds the answer, which question attention rarely identifies [@peng2026repairbudget]. RelayCaching reuses the KV that earlier agents produced while decoding and recomputes the sparse positions where the new prefix changes it [@geng2026relaycaching]. Other methods correct instead of recomputing: KVCOMM estimates the deviation of shared text across agents' prefixes from stored anchors [@ye2025kvcomm], AgentKVShift corrects every reused token of a memory unit by a shared offset [@pandey2026agentkvshift], and Kamera adds a low-rank correction, arguing that the failure is "a binding deficit written into the KV, not a boundary attention artifact" [@ma2026kamera], which agrees with what we find for edits. In all of these the documents never saw each other, and the query that reads them is new input. In a live edit the cached suffix did see the content that changed, which is what makes a recorded exposure available, and the reader is often itself stale.

**Position-independent caching and positions.** Prompt Cache reserves position ranges for reusable modules [@gim2024promptcache]. Block-Attention and TurboRAG fine-tune a model to accept independently encoded blocks [@ma2025blockattention; @lu2024turborag], and APE aligns parallel with sequential encoding without training [@yang2025ape]. Some systems store keys without rotation and apply positions at read time [@yang2025kvlink; @wang2025mepic; @ordonez2026minipic; @agarwal2025cachecraft]; others rotate stored keys [@yao2025cacheblend; @ma2026irminsul; @ma2026kamera; @liu2026redknot]. Re-rotating cached keys dates at least to llama.cpp's context shifting [@llamacpp3228] and is exact under rotary embeddings [@su2021roformer]. Dual Chunk Attention scores chunk-relative keys with differently positioned queries and merges the results in one softmax [@an2024dca], the kernel pattern query-side relocation needs. StreamingLLM assigns positions within the cache rather than in the original text [@xiao2024streamingllm]. Group position encoding trains a model to accept separate position ranges [@tong2025streaming]; our measurements of gaps concern models without such training.

**Prefix caching and attention-based selection.** vLLM manages the KV cache in pages and identifies each block by a hash chained over the blocks before it [@kwon2023vllm; @vllmapc], and SGLang organises cached prefixes in a radix tree [@zheng2024sglang]. Both reuse a cell only if everything before it is unchanged. RAGCache keeps the KV of retrieved documents as order-dependent prefixes across GPU and host memory [@jin2024ragcache], and Stream2LLM recomputes everything after the longest common prefix when streamed content changes [@bachkaniwala2026stream2llm]. Eviction policies such as H2O and SnapKV choose cache entries by the attention they receive [@zhang2023h2o; @li2024snapkv]; our reader attention is the same kind of signal, used to choose what to recompute rather than what to keep. The two quantities we combine are one step each of the attention-flow measures of attention rollout [@abnar2020rollout], and information-flow routes trace important paths from input to output in the same spirit [@ferrando2024ifr].

**Context management and multi-agent state.** Context managers in the style of MemGPT page content in and out of a bounded window [@packer2023memgpt], which produces exactly the interior edits studied here. Hashrope serves edits, snapshots and prefix identity from one persistent hashed rope [@syed2026hashrope], a data structure our segment versions could sit on. Merging branch caches has been studied as replicated state [@baquero2026merge] and as workflow operators [@sarker2026aaflow]; neither repairs merged state for the reader that consumes it. Event time, watermarks and late data are standard in dataflow systems [@akidau2015dataflow], and we borrow the watermark as a sealing point for model state. In-context unlearning asks a model to act as if training examples were absent [@pawelczyk2024icul]; Section 5.5 asks the analogous question of a repaired cache and its deleted content.
