## 6. A runtime for mutable contexts

An engine that serves stale cells has to know, after every edit, which cells are still exact, or it cannot say what it is serving. Sections 3 to 5 deal with one edit, while agents produce sequences of them: a tool result arrives late, an older one is cleared, two sub-agents' findings are merged, and the next input arrives in between. We built a small state model that keeps track of what is exact after any such sequence; Appendix G walks through an example.

**Segments over immutable cells.** A context is an ordered list of segments. Each has a key, such as a tool-call id, an order key that fixes where it belongs, and the cells of its tokens. Cells are never written after they are created, and they are created in only three ways: when a segment is encoded on arrival, when a repair recomputes some of its tokens, and when a sweep re-encodes it. Inserting, deleting or replacing a segment changes which segments are in the list and where, but moves and copies no cell, since relocation happens at read time (Section 3). Each encoding gets a **version**, a content address computed from its tokens and from the version and relative start of every segment that preceded it when it was encoded. A segment is **exact** if what now precedes it is what it read, and the **frontier** is the number of leading segments that are exact. This is the prefix-cache test applied per segment, much as vLLM applies it per block with chained block hashes [@vllmapc]. The difference is that stale segments stay in use at an offset, with a record of what they read and what they missed, until they are repaired or swept. A segment behind an edit that is later undone becomes exact again without being recomputed, and cells produced by a targeted repair get a fresh, unique version, so they are never mistaken for exact ones.

**Consistency levels.** A reader can be served at one of three levels. **Exact** means everything it depends on is behind the frontier, which is what a prefix cache provides and what Zhang and Yang require after a rollback [@zhang2026aborted]. **Repaired** means stale segments remain but a share of their tokens has been recomputed for this reader (Section 5). **Positional** means stale cells are reused as they are, the contract of Leyline's splice [@ma2026leyline]. The two weaker levels are temporary: a **sweep** re-encodes the stale segments from the frontier onward, left to right, in chunks that can be interrupted between segments, so the state becomes exact after work equal to one re-prefill of the stale part. That work can run off the latency path, for example while a tool call runs. A segment that arrives late is encoded on arrival at the place its order key gives it, and the segments after it are marked as missing a read. Advancing a watermark, the stream-processing promise that no earlier event will arrive [@akidau2015dataflow], seals the segments before it once they are swept, and a later event behind it is rejected instead of silently making sealed state stale. A fork shares every cell, and a merge takes the union of two branches' segments by key and refuses keys whose contents differ. Baquero and Brito showed that content-addressed KV fragments under union form a convergent replicated data type [@baquero2026merge], and AAFLOW+ defines fork and merge operators over KV state [@sarker2026aaflow]. Our additions are the exactness tracking, repair of merged state for the reader that consumes it, and a path back to the re-prefill. The implementation is about 300 lines of Python, tested in 64-bit floats for exact appends, cell sharing, order-independent arrival and merge, undo without recomputation, and watermark sealing.
