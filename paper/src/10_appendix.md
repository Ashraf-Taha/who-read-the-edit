## Appendix A. Relocation, micro-benchmarks and a cost model

**Drift of rewritten keys.** Leyline reports that moving its rotation to 32-bit floats accounts for most of one end-to-end gain and leaves the drift of chained rotations unmeasured [@ma2026leyline]. A 480-token cache is stored in a 16-bit format and shifted repeatedly by random steps of up to 64 positions. One arm rewrites the stored keys each time, computing the rotation in 32-bit floats and rounding the result back, the more accurate of the in-place options (keeping an unrounded copy and rotating it once by the total offset would avoid the accumulation, at twice the memory). The other arm never touches the stored keys and rotates the query by the accumulated offset. By shift invariance the correct output is the same at every step, so any change is numerical. The log-log slope of the keys' relative error against the number of rewrites, fitted over 13 counts from 1 to 4,096, lies between {{e0.slope_min}} and {{e0.slope_max}} for all six combinations of model and format; after {{e0.max_edits}} rewrites in bfloat16, {{e0.flip_bf16_min}} to {{e0.flip_bf16_max}} of next-token choices have changed. The flip rates rest on 256 token positions per cell and are rough.

**Micro-benchmarks.** Table {{#A:kernels}} times one attention call with Qwen2.5-1.5B's head shapes in plain PyTorch on the laptop GPU. Scoring keys grouped by offset costs {{e14.gather.min}} to {{e14.gather.max}} times plain attention over keys already at their positions, for 1 to 32 offsets, 1 or 64 new queries and 4,096 or 16,384 cached keys; scattering the offsets in runs of 8 keys changes this by at most a factor {{e14.fragmentation.max}}. Scoring every key with all $D$ rotated queries and keeping each key's own score, which avoids gathers, costs {{e14.select.d32.min}} to {{e14.select.d32.max}} times plain attention at $D = 32$; rotating every cached key on each call costs {{e14.rewrite.min}} to {{e14.rewrite.max}} times. A tiled pass with an online softmax and one running sum per earlier segment reproduces the materialised per-segment attention to within {{e14.exposure.error}} and takes {{e14.exposure.min}} to {{e14.exposure.max}} longer than the same tiled pass without the sums ({{e14.exposure.segments}} segments). The calls take milliseconds, far longer than reading their keys from memory would, so these ratios mostly reflect how many operations PyTorch dispatches; they bound the overhead of an implementation like ours and say little about a fused kernel.

{{table:kernels}}

**Counting offsets.** The number of distinct offsets $D$ sets how many times the query is rotated. The number $R$ of maximal runs of consecutive cells that share an offset sets how fragmented the key reads are, and $D \le R$. Without targeted repairs both stay small: each edit adds at most one run behind it, and each insertion also adds its own cells as a run, so $D \le R \le 1 + n_{\text{del}} + 2\,n_{\text{ins}}$. A targeted repair recomputes scattered cells at offset zero, a value the prefix already has, so it fragments runs without adding offsets. Fifty scattered recomputed tokens after one deletion leave $D = 2$ but up to 101 runs. Later edits move repaired and unrepaired cells alike. A cell's offset is therefore fixed by the encoding it came from (the original prefill, one of at most $e$ insertions encoded on arrival, or one of $r$ repairs, so $1 + e + r$ possibilities) and by which of the $e$ edits lie before it (at most $1 + e$ regions), which gives $D \le (1+e+r)(1+e)$. A sweep resets both counts to one.

**A cost model for a fused engine.** Table {{#A:costmodel}} estimates what repair would cost in an engine with fused kernels running at peak, for the shapes of Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen2.5-7B and two GPUs given only by their peak bfloat16 compute and memory bandwidth. Each pass over $m$ tokens is charged the larger of its FLOPs over peak compute and its bytes over peak bandwidth. FLOPs are two per weight per token plus $4Hd$ per layer for every query-key pair; bytes are the layer weights in bfloat16, read once per pass (once per 2,048 tokens for a re-prefill, as chunked prefill does), the keys and values read by each tile of 128 query rows up to its last position, and the keys and values written for every recomputed token. The context has {{e24.prefix}} tokens before the edit, and the reader pass covers {{e24.new_input}} tokens of new input. Small passes are memory-bound on the weights, which is why the share of a re-prefill falls with the suffix length and barely changes with the budget at 1,024 tokens. Real kernels run below peak and pay launch and scheduling costs that weigh most on small passes, so real shares will be higher than these. The script is `e24_cost_model.py`.

## Appendix D. The replication with hypotheses fixed in advance

The hypotheses were written into the experiment script and the project log before the run started, with the test for each:

- **H1.** At 10% of the suffix, rank fusion and the bridge score each leave lower KL than reader attention alone on every model (paired Wilcoxon over questions, Holm-corrected within each model).
- **H2.** With the edited document 8th of ten, reader-first repair (found segment, then exposure) leaves lower mean KL than rank fusion; with it 2nd or 5th, rank fusion leaves lower mean KL (two-sided paired Wilcoxon per stratum).
- **H3.** With two short notes in the suffix, the most-read-per-token rule still finds the question in most cases, the shortest-segment rule does not, and recomputing the found segment first leaves lower KL than recomputing the shortest one first.
- **H4.** Without repair, the stale state gives the deleted document's first sentence a higher log-probability than the re-prefill does, and a 10% repair aimed at the answer removes only part of that excess.

The cases are the main experiment's layouts on 36 questions per model, drawn with seeds not used before (16000 plus the lead). A smoke test on one case before the run already contradicted the first half of H3: the attention rule picked the note next to the new input. H1: {{e16.h1_summary}}. H2: {{e16.h2_summary}}. Table {{#D:hypotheses}} gives every verdict, Tables {{#D:decoy}} and {{#D:leak}} the data for H3 and H4, and Table {{#D:tokens}} the budgets in tokens. The decision rules were logged in the project's research log at 13:39 on the day of the runs, with the script's SHA-256 prefix 36af5e09d34d2af0, before the results of Qwen2.5-0.5B and SmolLM2-1.7B were examined, and were applied after the fact to Qwen2.5-1.5B, whose results had been seen. The log entry is reproduced unchanged in `experiments/preregistration.md` of the code release. The Qwen2.5-0.5B run was still finishing then (it ended two minutes later); its rows were being written to disk as it ran, and none had been read. The rules are: $\alpha = 0.05$, two-sided, per model, no pooling across models; H1 holds on a model if both comparisons favour the combined policy with Holm-adjusted p < 0.05; H2 holds if both strata favour the predicted policy with p < 0.05 and fails if either is significant the other way; H3 holds if the attention rule finds the question in more than half of the cases, the shortest-segment rule in fewer than half, and found-first beats shortest-first with p < 0.05, and fails if the attention rule finds it in half or fewer; H4 holds if the stale state's excess log-probability has an interval above zero and the repair's excess is smaller, and fails otherwise. Every other outcome is "not confirmed". The H4 rule was written for the percentile bootstrap intervals we used then; the tables report BCa intervals, under which the H4 verdicts are {{e16.h4.bca_agreement}}. {{e16.qwen1.5b.reused_start}} questions nonetheless also appear in other experiments' results (one of them in the first repair run); the last column of Table {{#D:hypotheses}} repeats H1 and H2 without them.

**Inserts.** On the main experiment's cases read as inserts (Table {{#E:insert_reader}}), recomputing the most-read segment first and then the tokens with the most reader attention leaves {{e2e.qwen1.5b.segment_reader}} and {{e2e.smol1.7b.segment_reader}} nats on Qwen2.5-1.5B and SmolLM2-1.7B, against {{e2e.qwen1.5b.bridge_qs4g}} and {{e2e.smol1.7b.bridge_qs4g}} for the product of reader attention with an exposure estimate from kept queries ($p$ = {{e2e.qwen1.5b.segment_reader_vs.bridge_qs4g.p}} and {{e2e.smol1.7b.segment_reader_vs.bridge_qs4g.p}}) and {{e2e.qwen1.5b.reader}} and {{e2e.smol1.7b.reader}} for reader attention alone. For an insert the cached suffix never saw the new segment. First-layer exposure is free and exact, because first-layer queries and keys depend only on token and position. A better estimate keeps each token's queries from when it was encoded and scores them later against the keys of the new segment, after moving them by the relative shift (Proposition 1). Per head, the mass a token would have put on the new keys is $\exp(s_{\text{new}})/(\exp(\lambda)+\exp(s_{\text{new}}))$, where $s_{\text{new}}$ is the log-sum of its exponentiated logits on the new keys and $\lambda$ the log normaliser kept from encoding. Keeping one query and normaliser per key/value group at four evenly spaced layers costs {{mem.qwen0.5b.sketch_share}}, {{mem.qwen1.5b.sketch_share}} and {{mem.smol1.7b.sketch_share}} of a 16-bit KV cache (Table {{#E:memory}}), and it is no longer needed when the reader segment is known. On the first repair run's cases the estimate agrees well with true exposure (Spearman {{e2b.qwen0.5b.rho.qs4g}}, {{e2b.qwen1.5b.rho.qs4g}} and {{e2b.smol1.7b.rho.qs4g}}), beats reader attention alone on all three models, and closes {{e2b.qwen0.5b.qs4g.gap_closed}}, {{e2b.qwen1.5b.qs4g.gap_closed}} and {{e2b.smol1.7b.qs4g.gap_closed}} of the gap between reader attention and true exposure (Table {{#E:insert_sketch}}).

## Appendix G. The runtime: an example and the workloads

**Table {{#G:runtime_example}}.** Four segments: a system prompt A (50 tokens), tool results B (200) and C (100), and a cached question D (20). Versions are written $a_0$, $b_0$, and so on; the number after a version is the offset of that segment's cells. What a segment read when it was encoded is the list of (version, start relative to it) pairs of the segments before it.

| Step | Segments: version, offset | Frontier | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encode in order | A $a_0$ 0 · B $b_0$ 0 · C $c_0$ 0 · D $d_0$ 0 | 4 | Ordinary prefill. |
| Delete B | A $a_0$ 0 · C $c_0$ −200 · D $d_0$ −200 | 1 | C and D keep their cells, read with one query rotation for offset −200. They read $b_0$, which is gone, so they are stale; the attention they had put on B is kept as their recorded exposure. |
| Put B back | A $a_0$ 0 · B $b_0$ 0 · C $c_0$ 0 · D $d_0$ 0 | 4 | B is encoded against A and gets $b_0$ again, which is what C and D read: exact without recomputation. |
| Insert E (80) after B | A · B · E $e_0$ 0 · C $c_0$ +80 · D $d_0$ +80 | 3 | E is exact. C and D never read $e_0$ and are marked as missing a read. |
| Repair 10% for the next input | A · B · E · C $c_0'$ · D $d_0'$ | 3 | Twelve tokens of C and D are recomputed at offset 0; C and D get unique versions and stay non-exact. |
| Sweep | A · B · E · C $c_1$ · D $d_1$, all at offset 0 | 5 | C and D are encoded again, left to right; their cells are the cold pass's. |

**Repair score in the runtime.** The token-level repair score is reader attention times what the token is known to lack: its recorded exposure to deleted content plus, for content it never saw, either the kept-query estimate of Appendix E or a flat prior equal to the mean recorded exposure.

*Out-of-order arrival* ({{e5v2.qwen1.5b.n}} cases). A question is followed by six documents with a canonical order that arrive in random order. Appending each document in arrival order is exact for that order but a different context: against the canonical order, KL is {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.append.kl}} and {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.append.same}} of answers are the same. Encoding each document on arrival at its canonical place gives {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.speculate.kl}} without repair, and a 10% repair brings it to {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.repair_sketch@0.1.kl}} with the kept-query estimate and {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.repair_flat@0.1.kl}} with the flat prior ({{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.repair_flat@0.1.same}} of answers unchanged). Reader-first repair is the wrong choice here ({{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.repair_sketch_first@0.1.kl}} with the kept-query estimate; lower KL than the same repair without it in only {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.sketch.0.1.first_vs_plain.win}} of cases, $p$ = {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.arrival.sketch.0.1.first_vs_plain.p}}). The question sits in the exact prefix, and the stale segments are documents that missed other documents, so the segment the new input reads most is not where the damage is. The exact baseline is in-order commit, which encodes each document as soon as all documents before it have arrived; when the last document lands it still has to encode {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.inorder}} tokens on average, against {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.wait}} for waiting for everything. Speculative encoding has only the last document left ({{e5v2.qwen1.5b.last}} tokens), then the reader pass and the repair: {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.repair_path}} tokens on the latency path against {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.inorder_path}}, with the caveat that scattered recomputation costs more per token than prefill (Section 5.6). A sweep to the exact state costs {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.sweep}} tokens more.

*Branch merge* ({{e5v2.qwen1.5b.n}} cases). Two branches fork from a shared prefix, each reads three documents and writes a note, and the branches are merged before a question is asked. Without repair KL is {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.merge.kl}}; a 20% repair brings it to {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.repair_flat@0.2.kl}} with the flat prior, {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.repair_sketch@0.2.kl}} with the kept-query estimate and only to {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.repair_flat_first@0.2.kl}} with reader-first repair and the flat prior ({{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.repair_sketch_first@0.2.kl}} with the kept-query estimate; lower KL in {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.flat.0.2.first_vs_plain.win}} of cases, $p$ = {{e5v2.qwen1.5b.merge.flat.0.2.first_vs_plain.p}}).

*Chained edits* ({{e12.qwen1.5b.n}} agent traces of Appendix E; Table {{#G:chained}}). Two tool results are cleared one after the other, with a repair for the answer turn after each. Stale state accumulates: without repair the first-token KL grows from {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.positional.1}} to {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.positional.2}}. The runtime's token-level repair, whose estimate for the placeholder is the flat prior, ends at {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.repair_repair.2}}; reader-first repair, which recomputes the closing thought first, ends at {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.repair_repair_reader_first.2}} (lower in {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.first_vs_plain.win}} of traces, $p$ = {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.first_vs_plain.p}}). This reverses the single-edit comparison, where token-level scores used the exposure recorded by an exact cold pass. In the runtime the second repair works from records the first repair left partly stale, and the flat prior gives every token of every stale segment the same estimated loss, so reader attention alone decides most of the ranking. A sweep between the two edits resets the first one's staleness, and a 10% repair after the second then ends at {{e12.qwen1.5b.chain.sweep_repair.2}}.
